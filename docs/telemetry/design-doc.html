Harness Attribution and Telemetry

How we tell whether the Claude SDLC harness is actually improving productivity, developer experience, and quality on the Common Platform — and what it costs to find out.

Status proposed Repo hmcts/cpp-claude Plugin hmcts-sdlc-orchestrator@1.2.0 Revision 2.1 — attribution moved out of git history; collector designed Feedback by —
01

Why this exists

The harness is initiative 2 on the continuous improvement roadmap, and its Q4 milestone is “standardise & measure”. Today we cannot do the second half. We can say how many agents and skills the plugin ships; we cannot say whether a PR that used them was faster, safer, or better than one that did not.

That gap matters in both directions. Without evidence, a genuine win goes unrecognised and unfunded — and a quality regression goes unnoticed until it reaches a courtroom. HMCTS assurance will reasonably ask for the second before accepting the first.

This is time-critical in one specific way. Attribution cannot be reconstructed after the fact. Every week the harness spreads without something recording which commits it produced is a week of data we can never segment. That is the only part of this design with a deadline attached, and it is the cheapest part to build.

What changed in revision 2. The first version of this design put an Assisted-By: trailer in the commit message. That was withdrawn: most CPP repositories are public, and a permanent, world-readable AI-assistance marker on the history of a criminal justice system is a liability we cannot retract once published. Attribution now lives in a local index keyed by commit SHA. Section 04 sets out the reasoning and what it costs; the trade is real and it is decision D3 below.

02

Goals and non-goals

Goals

  • Segment delivery and quality data into harness-assisted and unassisted work, using systems we already run.
  • Show which of the ~15 skills and 8 pipeline agents are actually used, so the dead weight can be deleted.
  • Measure how often a human gate rejects an agent's output — the only direct quality signal we have per agent.
  • Collect all of it without recording anything that could identify a developer or leak case data.

Non-goals

  • Individual performance measurement. Explicitly out of scope, and the design is shaped to make it difficult rather than merely discouraged.
  • Replacing Azure DevOps, Jira, or SonarQube. Those stay the systems of record; this adds the one field they lack.
  • Counting lines or percentage of AI-written code. Both reward volume over judgement and get gamed within a sprint. We will not collect them.
  • Real-time dashboards. Monthly review cadence is enough to act on.
03

How it works

Three small components, all local to the developer's machine, feeding one local store that a reporting script joins against git. No service to run, no network call on the hook path, and nothing added to anything that gets pushed.

The important property is that no component can break the developer's work. post-commit and post-rewrite run after the git objects exist and git ignores their exit status; pre-push only ever passes through the verdict of a hook that was already there; the telemetry hook always exits 0. The previous revision's prepare-commit-msg was the one piece that mutated a commit, and it is gone.

04

Component 1 — the attribution index

This is the part of the design that changed, and the part most worth reading closely.

Why not a commit trailer

The obvious design is a trailer in the commit message — Assisted-By: hmcts-sdlc-orchestrator@1.2.0. It is self-describing, needs no infrastructure, survives cloning, and is what most teams do. It was our first design.

It was withdrawn for one reason: the majority of CPP repositories are public. A permanent, world-readable AI-assistance marker on the commit history of a criminal justice system is a standing liability. It cannot be retracted — removing it would mean rewriting published history across roughly 145 repositories — and once published, what it signifies is decided by whoever reads it, not by us.

Do not solve this with an opaque marker. X-CPP-Pipeline: 3f2a is durable, needs no collector, and looks like meaningless internal noise. It is also concealment rather than absence, and its failure mode is worse than the one it avoids: HMCTS hid AI attribution behind a code is a better story than HMCTS used AI assistance, and the marker being obviously deliberate is what makes it one. If a marker is not safe to explain, it does not belong in a public repository.

What replaces it

Three git hooks record commit SHAs into the same local log as the usage telemetry. The commit that reaches the remote is byte-for-byte what the developer wrote.

HookRecordsWhy it is needed
post-commitcommit_recorded — SHA, harness version, stagesThe attribution itself. Requires a live session, which is what makes it an attribution rather than a guess
post-rewritecommit_rewritten — old SHA → new SHAA SHA is not stable. Without this, one rebase orphans the attribution — and tidying before review is the norm here
pre-pushbranch_pushed — tip SHA, remote refThe join key for PR-level metrics in Azure DevOps, which is where cycle time and change failure rate actually live

The reader walks the rewrite chain forward, so attribution stays attached to the commit rather than to the hash. Two consequences are worth stating plainly: post-rewrite and pre-push run without a live Claude session, because rebases and pushes routinely happen days after the work and requiring a session would break the chain exactly when it matters; and cherry-pick is not covered at all, because git does not fire post-rewrite for it. That last one is an undercount, and an acceptable one.

Recording is automatic, never remembered

Unchanged from the previous revision and still the most important property. A marker a developer has to remember would be missing perhaps a third of the time, and the missing third would not be random — it would skew toward rushed work, which is exactly the population the quality comparison needs. Absence has to mean “not assisted”, so absence has to be trustworthy. SessionEnd closes the attribution window, with an 8-hour TTL as a backstop for crashed sessions, so a manual commit made afterwards is not mis-attributed.

What this costs

Stated up front so nobody discovers it in a review:

  • The collector stops being optional. Trailers worked with zero infrastructure. This does not — until logs are collected centrally, a report covers one machine. This is why D4 has moved to the front of the queue.
  • A fresh clone shows 0% assisted, correctly and unhelpfully. The index does not travel with the repository.
  • Squash-merge becomes a join, not a fact. Azure DevOps builds the squash commit server-side, so there is no local SHA to catch. The route is PR → source commits → index, which works but is more fragile than reading a trailer off a merge commit.
  • No permanent provenance record. Asked in two years which code was AI-assisted, we could answer only for the retention window and only for machines that reported. If a disclosure obligation turns out to apply, this design does not discharge it — see D3.
  • PR tagging can no longer be a pipeline one-liner. With trailers, context-verify.yaml could grep the commits at build time. Now the signal has to reach ADO from the developer's machine, which means the collector has to exist first.

What we gain beyond the political point. The index is revocable — deleting ~/.cpp-harness removes every record, which makes “you can withdraw your data” a true statement rather than an awkward one. And the hooks no longer touch the commit at all, which removes the one component in the previous design that could have broken somebody's workflow.

05

Component 2 — the telemetry hook

harness-telemetry.sh is wired to SessionStart, SessionEnd, Stop, and PostToolUse on Skill|Task|Agent|Write|Edit. It appends one JSON object per line to a monthly local file. It never blocks and always exits 0 — telemetry must not be able to break a session.

EventEmitted onFeeds
session_start / session_endSession lifecycleWeekly actives, sessions per developer, repo coverage
skill_usedSkill toolPer-skill invocation counts — which of ~15 skills earn their keep
agent_usedTask/Agent toolPer-stage agent usage; pipeline entry and completion
artefact_writtenWrite under docs/pipeline/Pipeline completion rate
gate_decision/gate commandGate rejection rate by stage
commit_recordedpost-commit git hookThe assisted/unassisted split underneath every flow and quality metric
commit_rewrittenpost-rewrite git hookKeeps that split intact across amend and rebase
branch_pushedpre-push git hookJoin key for PR-level metrics in Azure DevOps

The per-skill breakdown is the highest-value line in that table and the one teams routinely skip. It is what tells us which skills to invest in and which to delete.

06

Component 3 — the gate command

At each of the six human gates, /gate <stage> <accept|edit|reject> [reason] records what the reviewer decided. Reasons come from a fixed vocabulary (incomplete, wrong-pattern, missed-standard, hallucinated, style, scope-creep, other); free text is rejected outright, because logs are retained and the PII rule applies to them too.

This is the only manual step in the design, and it is manual because gate outcomes genuinely cannot be inferred from tool calls. It is also the highest-value data we can collect: it reads in both directions. A requirements-analyst rejected 60% of the time is a prompt problem fixable in a week. A code-reviewer rejected 5% of the time may instead mean reviewers have stopped reading — the more dangerous failure, invisible in every other metric, and indistinguishable from excellence unless someone looks.

07

Component 4 — the reader

scripts/harness_metrics.py reads the event log, joins its recorded SHAs against git log across the workspace, and reports adoption, per-skill and per-agent usage, repo coverage, pipeline abandonment, gate rejection rate, and assisted-commit share. Standard library only; read-only; three output formats (terminal, markdown for the monthly review, JSON for a future collector).

It deliberately refuses to compute change failure rate, defect escape rate, PR review turnaround, cycle time, CI first-pass rate, and the SonarQube trend. Those need Azure DevOps, Jira, and Sonar. Rather than substitute a local proxy that would get read as the real thing, the report prints them in a “not derivable locally” table naming the API each one needs. Two readings are editorialised in the output for the same reason: a 0% reject rate warns that it may mean reviewers stopped reading, and an empty index prints the git config --get core.hooksPath check, since “not used” and “hooks not installed” are otherwise indistinguishable.

It also prints an index health block — commits recorded, rewrites followed, and how many recorded commits could not be found in the scanned history. That last number is the honest cost of keeping the marker out of the commit, and it has to be read before the assisted percentage is quoted anywhere: a high count means the percentage is an undercount, not that the harness is unused. Superseded SHAs are excluded from it, or every routine rebase would make the index look broken.

08

Component 5 — the collector

The one component that does not exist. This section proposes its shape so the question in D4 is a choice between concrete options rather than an open field.

Attribution now lives in a file on a laptop. Until those files are gathered somewhere, every number in section 07 describes exactly one machine — and a report that says “38% assisted” while covering 40 of 120 developers is not a small error, it is a different claim. That is the structural cost of revision 2, and the collector is what pays it.

What it has to do

Three things, in priority order. Get the local JSONL off the machine without ever sitting on the path of a commit. Reach Azure DevOps, so branch_pushed can be joined to PR source branches — that join is the only thing that lets cycle time, review turnaround, change failure rate and CI first-pass be split assisted against unassisted. And, third, not quietly rebuild at the ingest point the identifiability this whole revision exists to avoid. The third is the one that is easy to get wrong.

Where it runs

Nothing here needs to be procured or invented. Read off the programme's own terraform and the environment dashboard's deployment notes:

PieceProposalAlready on the platform
Event storeA new, dedicated Log Analytics workspace64 azurerm_log_analytics_workspace declarations; Azure Monitor private link scope in use
IngestLogs Ingestion API behind a small service on the mgmt gatewayazurerm_monitor_data_collection_rule already used; the env dashboard is the deployment pattern
Monthly jobScheduled Azure DevOps pipelineMDV-ADO-AGENTS-01, which already holds ADO, Jira and Sonar access
PresentationDashboards only, no new toolGrafana at grafana.mgmt01.dev.nl.cjscp.org.uk

A dedicated workspace, not an existing one. Retention, query access and purge all scope to the workspace. Sharing a platform workspace would mean anyone holding reader on it can read developer telemetry, and that the deletion promise cannot be kept without touching unrelated data. A workspace of its own turns “we hold this for twelve months, these named people can query it, and here is how it is deleted” from an assurance into a configuration.

Not Splunk, and not Application Insights. Splunk is the security and audit sink; routing developer-behaviour telemetry into it sends a signal we do not mean and would very likely make deletion impossible by design. Application Insights is built for application performance and gives weaker retention and purge control over what is, in substance, a research dataset.

Why a proxy rather than posting straight to Azure

A laptop cannot use Managed Identity, and the hard rule against SAS tokens, connection strings and account keys rules out every shared-secret route — so the developer authenticates as themselves, whichever design we pick. Posting directly to an Azure data collection endpoint would need no service at all, but it means the developer's identity is present at the front door and “we do not know who sent this” becomes a statement about which table we query rather than about what exists.

A small service in front of the workspace authenticates the developer, checks group membership, discards the identity and forwards under its own workload identity. The difference between not stored and not collected is exactly the difference this revision was written to protect, and rebuilding identifiability at the ingest layer by accident would be a worse outcome than never having moved attribution out of the commit. The env dashboard profiles at 100m CPU and 128Mi under two hundred concurrent users; this does strictly less work.

The shipper

A SessionEnd hook plus a daily catch-up, reading unshipped lines against a watermark. It is deliberately not called from the git hooks: a network call on the commit path would put a VPN drop or a slow endpoint between a developer and their own commit, which is the one property this design has protected throughout. The local JSONL stays the source of truth, which is also what keeps rm -rf ~/.cpp-harness an honest deletion rather than a partial one.

What the collector still cannot fix

Coverage remains a caveat, not a solved problem — a report covers the machines that reported, and “untracked” must never be read as “unassisted”. The mgmt gateway is internal, so telemetry arrives in bursts when people connect: an empty day is not evidence of an idle day, and the monthly job has to be written knowing that. Squash-merge and cherry-pick lose attribution exactly as described in section 12, and the collector does not change either.

Before building any of it. Run the pilot without a collector — five to ten volunteers, each running harness_metrics.py locally and sending the JSON by hand. It does not scale and is not meant to. What it buys is finding out whether these are the right ten metrics, and how badly real rebase and squash-merge behaviour degrades attribution in practice, before a quarter has been spent on a pipeline that measures the wrong thing accurately.

What is and is not collected

This section is the one most worth challenging. If anything here looks wrong for our context, say so before rollout rather than after.

CollectedNot collected
Event kind and UTC timestamp Prompt text — any of it
Session id (12 chars, opaque) File contents
Repo basename and branch name Absolute paths — /Users/<name>/… identifies a person
Skill / agent name, pipeline stage Developer name, email, hostname, hardware id
Gate decision + fixed reason code Free text, anywhere
Commit SHAs, and old→new SHA pairs Commit messages, diffs, or anything derived from them
Remote name and pushed branch ref Remote URLs
A random installation id — see below Case data, court references, PII of any kind
And nothing at all in the commit itself.

The installation id, and why it exists

Building the reader exposed a hole in the original design: with no identifying field at all, “weekly active developers” — the headline adoption metric — is uncomputable. Distinct sessions is not a substitute, because one person in five sessions looks identical to five people in one.

So the hook writes an install field: twelve random hex characters, generated once, stored in a local file, not derived from name, email, hostname, or hardware. It never leaves ~/.cpp-harness and never touches git. Deleting the file yields a new one and breaks nothing.

The honest framing: this makes counting people possible while keeping identifying them impossible without access to that person's laptop. It is a real, if minimal, widening of what we collect, and it is the item in this document most deserving of a second opinion.

10

Governance

This produces data about how people work, so the constraints on its use are part of the design, not an afterthought:

  • Aggregate only. Team and programme level. No per-developer breakdowns, no league tables, no performance-management use. The index carries no identity field specifically to make the wrong analysis harder to run by accident.
  • Announce before enabling. Developers should know attribution is recorded before their first tagged commit, not discover it in a dashboard.
  • Opt-out is honoured and untracked. CPP_HARNESS_TELEMETRY=off suppresses both the usage log and the attribution index. A high opt-out rate is a finding about trust in the programme and should be read that way, not engineered around.
  • Deletion is real, and should be documented as such. rm -rf ~/.cpp-harness removes every record held on that machine, retroactively — which is only true because attribution is not in the commit. Once a collector exists the same must hold centrally, and that is part of what D4 has to settle.
  • Consult the appropriate forum — departmental trade union side and staff engagement — before programme-wide rollout. Five minutes now is far cheaper than retrofitting consent onto six months of data.
11

Rollout

Phase 0

Baseline

Pull 3–6 months of pre-harness cycle time, change failure rate, and defect escape from Azure DevOps and Jira. Every comparison below is meaningless without it, and the history is cleanest now while adoption is still narrow.

Phase 1

Attribution first, one team

Hooks and telemetry on a single team, including at least one cpp-ui-* repo to prove the husky chaining across post-commit and pre-push. Confirm the merge strategy per repo — under squash-merge the commit-level numbers become a PR-level join.

Phase 2

Announce, then widen

Governance conversation — including the disclosure question in D3 — then the core.hooksPath line into the onboarding script.

Phase 3

Collection now blocking

Run the manual pilot first — five to ten volunteers exporting by hand — then build the collector as drawn in section 08, then the ADO PR-tagging job that joins branch_pushed to PR source branches. Until this exists a report covers one developer at a time — which under the new design is the difference between a metric and an anecdote. Destination, retention, query access, and deletion get decided before it is built.

Phase 4

Monthly review

Ten metrics, reviewed monthly against the measurement framework. Expect the three-month answer to be “adoption is uneven and two skills do most of the work” — that is a useful finding, and it tells us where the next quarter goes.

Decisions we would like your view on

Everything above is reversible. These six are the ones where an outside opinion changes what we build. D3 is the one this revision turns on.

The pseudonymous installation id

Without it we cannot count active developers at all. With it, a random opaque value exists that distinguishes one laptop from another. Is that trade acceptable, or should we accept a weaker adoption metric to collect strictly nothing?

Our lean Keep it, documented and opt-out-able. But this is the item we most want challenged on the privacy side.

Global core.hooksPath

It is the only mechanism that installs git hooks across 145 repos without touching each one, and we have made all three chain safely. The alternative is per-repo installation, which is honest but will decay.

Our lean Global, with the chaining hooks, piloted on a UI repo before anyone else adopts it.

Keeping every AI marker out of public git history — and whether something pulls the other way

We have assumed the risk runs in one direction: a permanent, public Assisted-By trailer on a criminal justice system's history is a liability, so we removed it, and accepted a collector dependency, a per-machine blind spot, and the loss of any durable provenance record to do it.

The assumption worth testing is whether an obligation runs the other way. ATRS is aimed at algorithmic decision-making rather than developer tooling, so it probably does not bite — but “probably” is doing real work in that sentence, and someone in the department will know. If disclosure is required, the question becomes where it lives (a service-level statement, a repo README, an ATRS record), not whether to hide it; a per-commit trailer would be an odd way to discharge it in any case.

Worth knowing either way: Co-Authored-By: Claude is already common in public repositories, including government ones, so the shock value may be lower than it feels. We did not think that general observation should override a specific read of CPP's exposure.

Our lean Keep the marker out, and treat any disclosure obligation as a separate, service-level statement rather than a commit-level one. Confirm before rollout — this is the question we would most like answered by someone with departmental standing.

Where should collected telemetry live, and for how long?

Section 08 now proposes an answer — a dedicated Log Analytics workspace, an ingest service on the mgmt gateway that discards the caller's identity, and a monthly Azure DevOps job — so the open questions narrow to three: retention period, who holds query and purge rights, and whether a dedicated workspace can be created in the right subscription. This still blocks: with attribution held locally, nothing above team-of-one scale works until the collector exists.

Our lean Build it as drawn in section 08, with 12-month retention, query access limited to named people who publish what they run, and a documented central deletion path that matches the local one. Run the manual pilot first.

Should stages record what ran, or what was accepted?

Currently the index records what ran. Recording what a human accepted at the gate is more meaningful, but depends on /gate being used consistently — which it may not be.

Our lean Keep “run” in the index; keep “accepted” in the gate events. They answer different questions.

Is the governance framing sufficient?

Aggregate-only, no identity, honoured opt-out, real deletion, consultation before rollout. Is that the right bar for HMCTS, and is the trade union side the right forum, or does this need a formal DPIA?

Our lean We do not know. This is a genuine question, not a rhetorical one — guidance welcome.
